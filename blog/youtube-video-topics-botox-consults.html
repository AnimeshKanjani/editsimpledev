12 YouTube video topics that bring in Botox consults

The questions patients research before booking Botox, and how dermatology and aesthetic practices can answer them on YouTube: 12 video topics, filming tips and the compliance basics.

Almost nobody books Botox on impulse. Before a new patient calls your front desk, they have usually spent weeks quietly researching: how much it costs, whether it hurts, whether they'll look frozen, and whether you are the injector they trust with their face. Most of that research happens on YouTube and Google, and most practices aren't there to answer it.

The good news is that patients ask the same questions over and over. Answer them on camera, clearly and honestly, and your videos keep working for you long after you've recorded them. Here are 12 topics we'd put on any injector's content calendar, what the patient is really asking, and how to film each one well.

Questions patients ask before they ever call

1. "What does Botox actually do, and what can't it do?"

Start with the basics, explained the way you would in a consult: it relaxes the muscles that cause expression lines, it doesn't fill volume, and it won't fix every line on the face. Patients trust the injector who tells them what a treatment won't do. Use a mirror or a simple face diagram rather than patient photos.

2. "Botox or filler: which one do I need?"

This is one of the most common points of confusion, and a great video to send people to before a consult. Explain dynamic versus static lines, and which areas usually call for which treatment. End by saying the honest answer depends on an in-person assessment, which is also the natural next step.

3. "Will I look frozen?"

The fear behind most hesitation. Talk about how you dose for natural movement, what a conservative first treatment looks like, and why a follow-up visit lets you fine-tune. Show your own face moving if you're comfortable; it's more convincing than any claim.

4. "Does it hurt, and what happens at my first appointment?"

Walk through the visit from check-in to walking out: the consultation, marking, the injections themselves, and how long it takes. A calm, step-by-step video takes the unknown out of booking, and first-timers watch these to the end.

5. "How long does Botox last, and when will I see results?"

Patients want realistic timelines: when it starts to work, when it's at its best, and when it typically wears off. Results commonly last around three to four months, but it varies from person to person, so say that plainly. This is also the place to explain why regular appointments tend to give more consistent results.

6. "Why is it priced per unit, and how many units will I need?"

Price is the question people are most nervous to ask on the phone. You don't have to publish a price list. Explain how your practice prices treatments, why the number of units differs between people and areas, and what's included. Clarity here filters out bargain hunters and attracts patients who value expertise.

Videos for specific concerns

7. "Forehead lines, frown lines and crow's feet: how each area is treated"

Patients search by the problem they see in the mirror, not by product name. One video per area (or one video with clear chapters) matches how people actually search, and each one can rank for its own query.

8. "Is 'preventative' or 'baby' Botox worth it?"

Younger patients are searching for this, and there is a lot of social-media noise around it. A balanced, evidence-minded take from a physician stands out, especially if you're willing to say when it isn't necessary.

9. "Can Botox help with jaw clenching or a wide jaw?"

Masseter treatment gets a lot of curiosity. Explain who it may help, what it involves and what to expect. Before filming, check the current labeling status for this use in the US and say clearly whether it's on- or off-label. Patients appreciate that kind of straightness, and it keeps you on solid ground.

10. "Botox for underarm sweating"

Many patients don't know a dermatologist can treat excessive sweating, and the people searching for it are highly motivated. Explain how a medical consultation for hyperhidrosis works and how it differs from a cosmetic visit.

Videos that build long-term trust

11. "Aftercare: what to do (and avoid) after your treatment"

Useful for new patients and existing ones. It reduces repetitive phone calls, and it's the video your staff will send most often. Keep it short and practical, then cut it into a few Shorts.

12. "Side effects, and what happens if something isn't right"

It feels counterintuitive to talk about bruising or a drooping eyelid on your own channel, but this is often the video that wins the booking. Explain what's common, what's rare, how you minimise the risks, and what you do if a patient is unhappy. Honesty here signals exactly the kind of injector people want.

How to make these videos actually bring in consults

  • Answer the question in the first 30 seconds. People clicked for an answer. Give it, then go deeper.
  • Say where you are. Put your city and practice name in the title or description and mention it naturally on camera. Patients book locally, and "Botox in [your city]" searches are where intent is highest.
  • Add chapters. Long videos with clear chapters help viewers jump to their question and help YouTube understand what each part covers.
  • Cut every long video into Shorts. A 10-minute answer usually holds three or four 30-second clips for Shorts, Reels and TikTok.
  • End with one clear next step. "If you're in [city] and want to know what's right for you, book a consultation" is enough. No hard sell.

What to keep out of your videos

  • No guarantees. Avoid promising specific results, how long they will last for a particular person, or "no downtime" for everyone. Results vary, and your medical board and the FTC expect your advertising to say so.
  • No patient information without proper written consent. Before-and-after photos, faces and stories all need written authorisation that covers marketing use. When in doubt, use yourself, a staff volunteer or diagrams.
  • Be clear about who is treating. State your credentials and who performs injections at your practice.
  • Remember it's a prescription medicine. Keep the tone educational, and remind viewers that the right plan comes from an in-person assessment.

Rules differ by state, so it's worth having someone in your practice review scripts against your state medical board's advertising rules before you publish.

Where to start

You don't need all 12 at once. Pick the three questions your front desk hears most often, record them in one sitting, and publish one every couple of weeks. A small library of honest, specific answers will do more for your consult numbers than a weekly stream of generic content.
